What is manufacturing sales analytics?

Manufacturing sales analytics brings enquiries, quotations, accepted orders and invoiced sales into a commercial review. The aim is to understand where demand comes from, how it changes and which actions deserve attention.

For a manufacturer, winning work also creates commitments for materials, people and machines. An increase in sales may be welcome, but the mix of work and the promised delivery dates determine what the factory needs to do.

Sales analysis gives owners, sales managers and operations managers a shared starting point. It can highlight a quiet customer, a change in ordering patterns or a growing order book that needs a closer capacity review.

Manufacturing sales KPIs worth reviewing

Choose measures that answer a business question. These are useful starting points for a sales review; the final scope of Fraction ERP Sales Analytics is still being developed.

Keep the definition and reporting period consistent when comparing sales performance.
Sales measureWhat it tells youWhat to check
Order intakeThe value of work accepted during a period.Use the order date and agree how cancellations and changes are treated.
Open order bookThe value of accepted work still outstanding.Use the remaining value after partial deliveries and review the expected delivery dates.
Invoiced salesThe value invoiced in a period.Use invoice dates and a consistent treatment of credit notes, tax and currency.
Quote conversionHow much quoted work becomes accepted work.Distinguish conversion by quote count from conversion by value; exclude superseded revisions and allow time for decisions.
Customer sales trendsHow individual customer demand changes over time.Compare equivalent periods and consider large one-off orders or seasonal patterns.
Customer concentrationHow much business depends on your largest customers.Use a consistent sales basis and period when calculating each customer's share.

Order intake and invoiced sales answer different questions

An order accepted in October might be manufactured in November and invoiced in December. Counting it as October order intake and December invoiced sales is useful, provided the two measures stay distinct. A fall in invoicing does not automatically mean the sales team has stopped winning work, and a rise in orders does not mean the cash has arrived.

Turn customer sales analysis into useful follow-up

A customer ranking is a starting point. The next step is to understand the change and agree an action.

Notice customers going quiet

Compare recent orders with a customer's usual buying pattern. A gap might reflect a project finishing, a delayed programme or work moving elsewhere. Use the evidence to start a conversation rather than assume the reason.

Understand growth and dependence

Identify customers placing more work, then review whether that growth is repeatable. Consider how a change in one large account would affect the order book and the factory's workload.

Review quotes that need attention

Look at open quotations alongside their age and customer context. Agree who should follow up, which opportunities remain active and which outcomes need recording in your CRM.

Consider the value of the work

Sales value alone does not establish profitability. Review the work alongside reliable costing evidence, production effort and delivery demands before deciding which business to pursue.

Connect sales performance to production decisions

More orders help when the business can fulfil them. Bring the sales conversation together with material availability, factory capacity and delivery commitments.

Check capacity before promising dates

Order value is not a measure of machine hours or operator availability. Use production scheduling and capacity planning to review the operational effect of accepted work and proposed delivery dates.

Keep order review part of the process

Confirm the requirements, engineering questions and supply constraints before acknowledging an order. A manufacturing contract review gives the team a deliberate opportunity to resolve issues early.

Build a repeatable manufacturing sales review

Use a small set of agreed measures and turn the findings into decisions your team can follow through.

  1. Agree the definitions. Decide which order and quote statuses count, how dates are selected and how currencies and revisions are handled.
  2. Compare like with like. Review equivalent periods and separate repeat demand from large one-off projects.
  3. Investigate the records. Check the customers, quotes and orders behind a change before drawing conclusions.
  4. Bring in operations. Discuss capacity, materials and promised dates alongside the sales position.
  5. Assign the next action. Give customer follow-ups and internal checks an owner, then review progress at the next meeting.

Accurate customer records and up-to-date quote and order statuses make the review more useful. Existing manufacturing reports, ERP dashboards and KPIs provide supporting views of the wider business.
